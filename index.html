A gazetteer


Nobody has been there, so the names are provisional.



  
The Hash ###

  
The bright uplands, wherever the light happens to fall. The Hash is not a place so much as a time of day;
      its inhabitants, if any, would have to keep walking east to stay in it.

  
Oland ooo

  
Middle country. Rounded hills, each the same size as every other, arranged in rows. Reputed to be pleasant.

  
The Colons :::

  
Land at dusk. Everything here is about to be explained, and then the planet turns and it isn't.

  
The Tilde Sea ~~~

  
Open water in full sun. The waves are all one wavelength, which makes navigation easy and poetry difficult.

  
The Flats ---

  
The same sea, later in the afternoon, having given up on waves.

  
Full Stop Deep ...

  
The night ocean. Sentences end here. Ships crossing it report a long pause, then land, or morning,
      whichever comes first.

  
The Asterisk Caps ***

  
Ice at both poles, each flake a footnote to something nobody wrote down. On the dark side they
      dim to plus signs, which is the only arithmetic the planet does.

  
The At @

  
The moon. It has one side and shows it to everybody. Goes around behind the world at regular
      intervals, for reasons of its own, and comes back looking exactly as it did. Not on any map,
      being the only thing here that is somewhere else.

  
The Exclamations !

  
Flags, left by visitors. The world holds nine at most; plant a tenth and the oldest quietly falls
      over. Each is kept only by the person who planted it, so no two visitors see the same claims,
      and every territorial dispute here is settled in advance by nobody else knowing about it.





  
The Apostrophes ' . `

  
The stars. Unlike the flags, they are the same for everyone, having been dealt once from a
      fixed deck. They do not turn with the world, or at all. Each one blinks now and then, to show
      that it could have been otherwise. Occasionally one of them leaves; the count is not affected.

  
The Caret ^

  
A ship, and the only one. It has no port, there being no ports, and no cargo, there being nothing
      to want. It sails until it sees land, turns away, and sails until it sees some more. The crew
      regard this as a route. It is the one thing on the world that goes anywhere the world is not
      already taking it, and it is hard to find, which is how you know it is small and not far away.

  
The Shade :.

  
An eclipse, of which there is one a month, the At being punctual. When it comes out in front it
      stands in the light, and a small round night crosses the day from west to east, taking the colour
      out of whatever it passes over. The Hash becomes the Colons for a moment and the Tilde Sea goes
      as quiet as the Deep. Nobody underneath has ever looked up, there being nobody. The Caret has been
      under it many times and each time has lit a lamp that cannot be seen from here.

  
Elsewhere ?

  
Everything that is not the world. There is a great deal of it and none of it is listed.
      Travellers who insist on going are shown the way and, shortly afterwards, the way back.

  
The Percent %

  
A bottle, when there is one. There is only ever one, and only for those who were sent it or who
      threw it in themselves. It goes where the water goes, which is east, and around anything in the way.
      The Caret has passed it several times and has never once stopped, being a ship and not a postman.

  
The Dittos " " "

  
Weather. Cloud comes over from the west in rows, each mark meaning the same as the one above it, which
      is what the mark is for. It has no opinion about coastlines and crosses the Hash and the Tilde Sea
      alike, so that for a while there is no telling which is underneath; the Caret goes in at one side and
      is not seen until it comes out at the other, and neither is a flag, though the flag is still there.
      It can only be seen by day. Whether there is cloud at night is a question for someone standing under it.

  
The Commas , ; ,

  
Small, curled, and fastened to the bottom, where they wait for the sentence to go on. They are under
      water nearly always and are uncovered only at the lowest of the ebb, for about as long as it takes
      to draw breath, which is what they are for. The only life so far reported. They have not confirmed it.




The seas, having names, now also have a forecast.


The inhabitants, having none, nevertheless have a language, and it has a phrasebook.


Nobody has been there. For a visitor prepared to be very small, that can be arranged.


There is no border, but there is a desk at it, and arrivals are asked to
   declare themselves. Nobody has yet been turned away, or seen.


There is a speaking tube round the back, behind the page, where the console is kept. Those who know how to get
   there may address the world directly, as world, and it will answer. It asks world.help of nobody else.


The world may be turned without touching it. Tab to it and it is in your hands: the arrows turn it,
   shift turns it further, space holds it still and lets it go, home puts it back where
   longitude starts, and enter plants a flag in the exact middle of whatever is facing you.


The Apostrophes have names. Point at one, anywhere that is not the world, and the telescope will give it:
   seventy in all, the same seventy for everyone, taken from the vocabulary of printers because the sky is mostly margin.
   Nobody has learned them. The ones standing behind the world cannot be asked, and are thought to prefer it.


For those who would sooner be told than point seventy times, the sky has been taken down and
   laid flat as a chart, names and all. A few of the stars have been joined up into figures.
   The stars were not asked, and have not moved closer together as a result.


The world has an inside, like anything. A hole was sunk at the middle of the picture to see what the
   punctuation is standing on, and the hole is still open. Mind the edge; it is all edge.


The world is on the syllabus. Nobody teaches it, there being nobody who has been, but it is examined all
   the same, and candidates may sit the paper. Bring a pen. The ticks are not kept.


The world can be played for. It has been cut round the middle and laid out straight, thirty squares
   from the post to the post, and two visitors with one die may race once round it. The sea
   does most of the moving. A visitor alone may play both hands, and will win, and should not read much into it.


The Caret is the only ship and is nevertheless mislaid from time to time. When it is, there is
   a search, conducted by ear, and anyone may join it. It has always turned up so far.


The Caret keeps to a route, or says it does, and a route implies a board. One has been put up,
   with flaps, listing the next six sailings. They are all the same ship. None is late.


The Caret has a crew, who have not been seen, and the crew have a song, which until now had not been heard.
   It has been taken down, nine verses and the tune. Visitors are expected to join in on the even lines.


The Caret also keeps a log, in pencil. A stretch of it has been copied out as a track,
   the kind a chartplotter or a walking map will open, with the world's degrees left exactly as they were. Laid over your
   own planet it runs through a good deal of dry land. It was all sea at the time. The ship is not at fault; the planet is.


The world does not print. Ask a printer for it and what comes out is the map, which until then nobody has drawn.


The map is flat, and the world is not, and there is a remedy for that involving scissors. It comes
   as a kit: twelve pieces, a moon, a ship, and one flag. Glue is not supplied. Neither is the turning.

   control-c. The weather stays behind, being difficult to pipe. Read it first; one should, with worlds.


Paper tears, and a terminal is cold. For those who would rather have a world they can drop without
   consequences there is a pattern: three colours, seventy-two stitches round the middle, and an
   orange's worth of stuffing. It is the first version with an inside that anybody put there on purpose.


The world comes in two editions and does not say which you have. Visitors who keep their machines dark
   see it by night, lit from the side. Those who keep them light get it on paper, in ink, where the Hash is simply
   the part with the most ink on it and the Apostrophes are flaws in the stock. It is the same world. Changing
   your mind changes the edition at once, without a reprint, which is more than paper has managed before.


The world cannot leave, but its address can, and when the address is handed on somewhere that shows
   pictures, a likeness goes ahead of it. It was taken once, on a clear afternoon, and does not turn.
   The At is in it. The Caret is not, having been asked to hold still. People who have seen only the likeness
   have seen the world at its best, and are advised that it is also sometimes cloudy, and usually later.


The world may be kept. A browser, asked in whatever way that browser likes to be asked, will put it on a
   shelf with the other things you own, under a small picture of itself, and it opens from there
   without an address, like something that lives with you. It has also been given a keeper, who sits behind the page
   and saves a copy of everything that goes by. When the line is down the keeper hands the copies back, and the world
   turns as before, a little out of date, which it was already. The Caret does not notice. It has never had a line.






Postcards


There is no post office, and nothing leaves. But the world will hold still for a picture.










Bottles


Nothing leaves; the postcards were clear about that. But a note can be put in the sea here and turn up
   in the sea of whoever is given the address, it being the same sea. The note is not kept anywhere.
   It travels in the address itself, folded small, which is the only way anything has ever got out.











Listening


It makes no sound, there being nothing for it to make a sound in. But each kind of place has been
   assigned a note, and three of them pass under the middle of the picture at any moment: one in the
   north, one on the equator, one in the south. Dragging the world plays it faster, though not better.








The tide


The At goes round and the sea goes after it, as seas do, without ever arriving. To find out how far, a post
   was driven into the shallows at the place where longitude starts, and seven marks were cut in it. The sea
   heaps up underneath the At and again on the side farthest from it, so the post is wetted twice for every time
   the At comes over. Dragging the world drags the post through the tide, which the tide does not mind.









From above


The world is only ever shown from the front, that being where the visitor was put. There is one other
   place to stand, which is straight over the top of it, looking down. From there it is a wheel and not a ball.
   The ice is the hub. The light comes in from the lower left, as it always did, and the top of the picture is
   the back of the world, which from the front has to be taken on trust.





You are at the bottom, marked, and have not moved. The At can be followed the whole way round from here,
   including the part where it used to vanish. Only the upper half of the world shows; whatever is in the lower
   half is underneath, where it is assumed to be carrying on.






The census


Everything on the world is counted, and since the world is made of nothing else, the count is complete.
   It is taken of the side facing you, the back being difficult to enumerate from here, and it is taken again
   about once a second, which no other administration has managed. Residence is by appearance. A square of
   upland is entered under the Hash while the light is on it and under the Colons when it is not, without having
   moved, and no objection has been received.





The At is not in the returns, being non-resident. Cloud is counted as cloud, and whatever is under it is
   not counted at all, on the principle that the enumerator did not see it. The figures are correct at the time
   of reading and at no other time. Drag the world and they are revised without complaint.






The calendar


A day is one turn, and takes about as long as reading this sentence slowly. A month is one trip of
   the At around the back and out again. The week has seven days, named for what the world is made of,
   and no weekend. Time began when you arrived. Dragging the world moves the date, in either direction;
   this is the only place where that works.



None of which fits in a diary, a day here being over before it can be written down. So the
   observances have been slowed to your pace and put in a form your own calendar will take:
   the almanac, eleven occasions, repeating. Subscribe and the Shade will cross your month
   as well, at noon on the fifteenth, for four minutes. You will be shown as free throughout. Everyone there is.






While you were out


The calendar starts when you arrive, which is flattering and not true. The world was turning before
   and went on turning after. It keeps no record of this. You do, on your own machine, without having
   been asked: one line, saying when you were last seen. What follows is worked out from that.







Company


It was given out that there is one world. That holds for as long as it is open in one window. You have
   it open in more than one, and each of them has been turning on its own account, with its own afternoon,
   under the impression that it was the only one. They have now been introduced. This is the other, as seen
   from here: small, because it is some way off, and a different colour, because it is not yours.





The flags are the same on both, flags being kept by the visitor and not by the world. The time of day is
   not, since time began in each when you arrived there. A world that is not being looked at stops turning
   and says nothing about it afterwards; from here, for once, it can be caught at it. Over there, this one
   is the small one.






The crossword


The world has been set as a puzzle, which it already was. Ten lights, as the squares are called by people
   who set these, and they are the only lights here that can be put in by hand. Every answer is somewhere
   above. Looking is not cheating; it is the only geography there is.

Questions nobody has asked


There is no enquiries desk, and so far no enquiries. The answers have been prepared anyway, to save time later.
   Each is kept shut until wanted. A question mark is Elsewhere, as the gazetteer has it; opening one turns it
   into a full stop, which is the Deep. That is roughly what being answered is like.



  How big is it?
  
About as big as it looks. This is unusual in a world and has been remarked on. Measured in its own
     characters it is a few dozen across; measured in anything else it depends how far back you are sitting.





  Why does it turn?
  
Nobody has stopped it for longer than they could hold a key down. The better question is why it turns
     east, and the answer is that east is defined here as the way it turns.





  Where does the light come from?
  
Up and to the left. There is nothing there. It has been looked for, with the telescope, and what was found
     was margin, and some stars named after it. The light arrives regardless.





  Is it safe to drag?
  
Yes. It has been dragged a great many times, in both directions, and no coastline has come loose. The Caret's
     crew feel it as weather. The Commas do not feel it at all, or have not said.





  What happens to it when I close the page?
  
Nothing that can be checked. It will be here when you come back, and will tell you how long you were gone,
     and will be right about everything except whether it missed you.





  Can I live there?
  
There is room. There is no air, fresh water, soil, or third dimension, and the ground is a different
     colour every time the sun moves. Applicants who have read this far and still wish to are advised to plant a
     flag and stand very near it.





  Who is in charge?
  
The post is vacant and has been since before the post existed. The At is the most senior object, being
     highest up, but has never given an instruction. The Caret has a captain, presumably, whose authority extends
     to the Caret, and who has used it only to turn away from land.





  Is there another one?
  
Every visitor has one, and they are all this one. They differ only in their flags, their bottle, and what
     day they think it is. Two visitors comparing worlds will agree on the stars and on nothing they did themselves.





  What comes after hello?
  
World. After that, by the look of things, quite a lot; but it was all put there afterwards, and none of
     it was promised. The greeting was complete when it was made. Everything since is the world answering.





  Why is this question here?
  
To make ten. Nobody asked for ten.








Colophon


The world is set in whatever monospace the machine had in the case, twelve pixels
   to the body and set solid, which is the printers' way of saying that nothing was put between the lines.
   Nothing could be; the coast would have come apart. The words round it are in Georgia where Georgia was to
   be had, and otherwise in the nearest serif that answered.


It has not been measured, the rule being kept on the other side of the glass.
   It is printed light on dark or dark on light, as the reader's own lamp dictates.


No type was cast for it. Every sort was in your case already, on your side of the glass, before the world
   was thought of. It was only a matter of putting them in order and then moving them sixty times a second,
   which is where compositors of the old school would have asked for more money. The Apostrophes, being named
   for the margin, are pleased to find themselves at last in a book that has one.
