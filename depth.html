In the round

The world is a ball. So far this has had to be taken on trust.

Everything else here is flat, including the parts that turn. The page has two directions and the world was drawn in both of them, and the third was left with the visitor, who has plenty. Below is the one picture in which it has been put back. It does not look like the world. It does not look like anything; it is a field of sorts, the same sorts, set in columns that very nearly repeat. The world is in the nearly.

  1. Two Ats stand over the field. There is one At, as the gazetteer is firm about, so one of them is yours to remove.
  2. Look at them, and then stop looking at them. Look through the glass at something a good way behind it, a wall, or tomorrow, and let the two drift until there are three.
  3. Hold the middle one steady. Then lower your eyes into the field, without hurrying and without focusing, which is harder than either.

The world comes up out of the page, round, about an orange across, with the sea lying back behind it. The At is up and to the right, standing off the surface, where it always said it was.

Setting it again deals fresh sorts and buries the same world under them; some visitors find one field easier than another and nobody knows why. Giving up is allowed. It shows what was there, drawn the ordinary way, in the two directions the page admits to. It is not the same, as those who managed the other will tell you at length.

The trick is an old one from your world, where it was done with dots and sold in shopping centres. It works because you have two eyes and they disagree, slightly, about where everything is, and something behind them has spent your whole life settling the argument and calling the result distance. Here the argument has been rigged. A sort that ought to come round again twelve places on comes round at eleven, or ten, or eight, and the settlement is that part of the page must be nearer. Nothing is nearer. The glass is flat. You are supplying the entire third direction yourself and the world is letting you.

The Caret is in the picture, as it happens. It is one sort wide and raised by one, off the lower left, which is below what any two eyes will agree on. It was not asked to hold still this time. It did not need to be.

Visitors with one eye, or two that have agreed to differ, get the ball by giving up, and are reminded that everybody else was getting it that way until now. The flat one is where it was, turning.
